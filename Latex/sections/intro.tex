\chapter{Introduction}

Computer architecture is the study of how a computer is organised and how its components work together during the execution of a program. In an undergraduate computer science degree, it usually appears only after students have already spent time with programming, discrete mathematics, and smaller hardware abstractions, establishing a foundation before they begin looking more closely at how software is carried out by the machine \cite{tcdComputerScienceModules,tcdComputerArchitectureI,uarizonaBsStructure,uarizonaCsc252}. What makes the subject distinctive is that it asks students to connect those earlier experiences with the behaviour of the machine itself, moving from writing programs to reasoning about registers, control, memory, and instruction flow.

It is also where the subject often starts to feel harder than what came before. Students are no longer working only with source code or with isolated components, but with instruction fields, registers, ALU behaviour, memory access, control decisions, and program counter behaviour that all have to make sense together. Traditional teaching builds toward this carefully, and for good reason: lectures, whiteboard derivations, traces, diagrams, and simulators all contribute something useful. Even so, many students reach a point where they can name the parts of a datapath and still cannot clearly explain what is happening when a particular instruction runs. The problem is less about missing facts than about learning to see execution as a connected process rather than as a labelled picture. Studies of architecture teaching report the same pattern from different angles. Porter et al. found that students in computer architecture courses often underperformed instructor expectations on supposedly basic concepts \cite{porter2013evaluating}, while Yehezkel et al. argue that learners can otherwise end up treating the machine too much like a black box rather than forming a viable mental model of its behaviour \cite{yehezkel2007contribution}. Tan et al. write that the subject is often perceived as ``dry, tough, and complex'' and that students struggle because they are ``unable to visualize these multiple-step procedures'' \cite{tan2023vr}; earlier work likewise describes architecture learning as difficult and, at times, unmotivating under conventional presentation \cite{tlili2016improving}.

This dissertation grew out of seeing that teaching problem repeatedly during undergraduate teaching-assistant work in computer organisation, where the most useful intervention was often not another verbal explanation but a slower walkthrough that drew over the datapath, followed the instruction step by step, and made each decision visible. The project presented here takes that teaching pattern as its starting point and asks whether a virtual reality environment can serve as a useful supplement at precisely that point of difficulty.

The prototype therefore deliberately does not attempt to replace lectures, diagrams, or conventional simulators, and it is not presented as a cycle-accurate machine model. Instead, it focuses on a selected set of single-cycle MIPS instructions and on a bounded set of learning tasks: decoding the instruction, selecting the correct operands, following the path of execution, making control-related choices, and reasoning about the resulting architectural state. To support different stages of learning, the environment is organised around three modes, Learning, Practice, and Test, which gradually reduce guidance while keeping the same overall lesson structure.

\section{Research Question and Scope}

Within that bounded scope, the dissertation is guided by the following question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{To what extent does an immersive virtual reality-based learning environment improve students' understanding of abstract computer architecture concepts compared to traditional teaching methods?}
\end{quote}

The aim is not to argue that virtual reality is broadly superior to other teaching methods, but to examine whether this particular prototype, used in this particular teaching context, offers enough educational value to justify the design and the study. For that reason, the evaluation is treated as exploratory and formative rather than as a final controlled proof of superiority over traditional approaches.

\section{Reader's Guide}

The remainder of the dissertation is organised as follows. Chapter 2 places the project in context by reviewing the background and related work, with particular attention to computer architecture learning, virtual reality in computer science education, and the practical limits of existing teaching support for first-pass mental-model building. Chapter 3 then sets out the methodological frame for the dissertation, including the research question, hypotheses, learning outcomes, study design, instruments, analysis plan, and ethical boundaries.

Chapter 4 explains the design of the learning environment itself, showing how those aims were translated into pedagogical choices, environment structure, interaction design, and the main abstractions used to make datapath behaviour teachable in VR. Chapter 5 describes the implementation of the prototype. Chapter 6 presents and discusses the results, and Chapter 7 closes the dissertation by returning to the research question, summarising the main contributions, and identifying directions for future work.
