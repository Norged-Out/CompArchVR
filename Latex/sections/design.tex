\chapter{Design}

The previous chapters established the teaching difficulty addressed by the project and the bounded way in which it was evaluated. This chapter explains how that difficulty was translated into a learning environment. The design problem was to decide which parts of instruction execution needed to become visible, which decisions should be left in the learner's hands, which abstractions could clarify the process without distorting it beyond use, and how far the whole system could be narrowed while still addressing the original educational concern in a serious way.

As outlined in Chapter~2, computer architecture is ordinarily taught through a gradual build-up that begins with logic and simpler hardware ideas before moving toward datapaths, control, and instruction execution \cite{tcdComputerArchitectureI}, and the prototype was therefore designed to support the later transition from recognition to explanation.

\section{Learning Outcomes and Design Scope}

The prototype was designed around a specific set of learning outcomes rather than around the broader and vaguer ambition of helping students ``understand computer architecture.'' Those outcomes were used to decide what the environment should teach, what kinds of learner action it had to preserve, and where the project had to stop. \\
By completing the VR experience, learners should be able to

\begin{enumerate}
\item Decode the relevant fields of a supported single-cycle MIPS instruction;
\item Explain the roles of the major datapath components involved in instruction execution;
\item Trace an instruction and its data through the major stages of a single-cycle datapath;
\item Select the correct operands and control-facing choices needed to execute a supported instruction;
\item Predict the architectural state changes caused by that instruction;
\item Compare how different instruction classes change datapath behaviour; and
\item Complete a supported instruction walkthrough with reduced or no guidance.
\end{enumerate}

These outcomes define an educational progression: first making sense of the instruction as an encoded object, then recognising the components and stages through which it travels, then reasoning through the choices that govern its execution, and finally carrying that reasoning forward with less support. The structure of the environment follows that sequence closely, which is why the lesson path, mode progression, and supported instruction set are all tied to the same small cluster of tasks.

The prototype does not claim to teach the whole of assembly programming, full digital-logic construction, pipelining, hazards, or the entire content of an undergraduate architecture course. It is also not intended to function as a comprehensive MIPS simulator or a general digital-logic tutor. It concentrates on selected single-cycle instruction walkthroughs because that is the point at which the educational problem can be made concrete without the system collapsing under its own breadth. A broader instruction set or curriculum target would have weakened the coherence of the build and blurred the claims that could reasonably be made about it.

A prototype built around only one clean example would have made the lesson easier to stage, but it would also have hidden the fact that different instruction classes activate the datapath differently and demand different forms of reasoning from the learner. The final scope therefore had to be broad enough to cover arithmetic, immediate, memory, branch, and limited jump behaviour, while remaining narrow enough for the lesson loop to stay legible and stable throughout the participant study.

\section{Pedagogical Position}

\subsection{From Ordinary Teaching to Supplementary Support}

The project was conceived from the beginning as a supplement to ordinary teaching rather than as an alternative to it. Lectures, tutorials, whiteboard derivations, simulators, and textbook diagrams already do several things extremely well: they provide sequence, terminology, worked examples, and a stable reference image of the machine. As Chapter~2 argued, the relevant difficulty appears only after that earlier exposure has already taken place. The prototype therefore assumes prior exposure to the topic and takes its place after that exposure, where it can reinforce, clarify, and slow down the reasoning rather than trying to replace the earlier stages of instruction.

That role shaped the tone of the whole environment. The system was meant to provide a more guided and more tangible form of walkthrough at the stage where diagrams and explanations have already introduced the topic but have not yet settled into a stable mental model. The design is closer to guided tracing on a whiteboard than to an open simulation or unguided exploratory world.

\subsection{Guidance, Progression, and Independence}

The pedagogical model is built around supported progression. Learning mode slows the process down, supplies explanation at the relevant moment, and keeps the learner oriented as the instruction advances through the datapath. Practice mode asks for more independent decoding and more deliberate decision-making while preserving the same overall route. Test mode strips back much of the remaining support and asks whether the learner can now carry the reasoning through with less intervention. This structure follows a pattern already visible in related architecture and educational game work, where tutorials and strongly supported tasks precede stricter or more independent activities \cite{tlili2016improving,tan2023vr}.

A student who can complete a task only while being coached through every step has not reached the same point as a student who can reconstruct the same reasoning more independently; at the same time, a learner who is denied support too early may never build the underlying structure in the first place. The three-mode arrangement reflects that balance by letting support recede gradually rather than treating explanation and independence as mutually exclusive states.

\section{Representational Strategy and Abstraction}

\subsection{Why Abstraction Was Necessary}

The central representational problem arose from a mismatch between hardware behaviour and teachable experience. A textbook single-cycle datapath carries out its work as one overall cycle whose internal activity is largely hidden, combinational, and parallel. Fetch, decode, operand selection, ALU behaviour, memory access, write-back, and determination of next program-counter can be named separately for teaching purposes, yet they are not experienced by the hardware as six clear, sequential teaching moments of the kind a learner usually needs before the process becomes intelligible.

For that reason, the design could not simply imitate the diagram and hope that immersion alone would close the gap. If every internal relationship remained as compact and as hidden as it is in the textbook picture, the environment would inherit much of the same first-pass difficulty that motivated the project in the first place. Work on VR in computer science education and immersive information design points in the same direction: the educational value of the medium depends on whether it clarifies the conceptual structure of the task without overwhelming the learner \cite{janani2026virtual,cook2019challenges,korkut2023visualization}. The abstractions used in the prototype were therefore justified by teachability before they were justified by realism.

\subsection{Preserving Reasoning Rather Than Literal Timing}

The guiding decision was to preserve the reasoning structure of instruction execution even when the literal timing and invisibility of the hardware had to be relaxed, which in practical terms meant treating the familiar phases of Fetch, Decode, Execute, Memory, Write-Back, and Program Counter Update as teaching phases rather than as claims about separate clocked steps in a real processor. The prototype thus remains grounded in the logic of a single-cycle datapath, but, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:datapathToVrTranslation}, it presents that logic in a form that can be followed, revisited, and acted upon by a human learner.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{DatapathToVrTranslation.png}
\caption[Datapath-to-VR translation overview]{Overview of how the traditional single-cycle datapath was translated into the routed VR environment. The figure shows how major textbook datapath regions were reorganised into learner-facing stations so that instruction execution could be followed as a visible sequence without implying separate hardware clock stages.}
\label{fig:datapathToVrTranslation}
\end{figure}

Several of the prototype's most visible abstractions follow from that choice. Fetch becomes a simpler visible acquisition and transfer event rather than a silent response to the program counter. Decode is given an explicit learner-facing role rather than being left as internal field interpretation and control generation. Memory use, write-back, and program-counter consequences each receive enough space to become legible parts of the execution story. These changes retain the underlying datapath relationships while redistributing them into a form that the learner can follow sequentially.

\subsection{Selected Representational Choices}

The physical register bank and the later use of ``datapackets'' offer the clearest example of this strategy. During decode, the learner benefits from seeing source and destination registers as stable architectural locations that must be identified correctly. Once the lesson moves beyond that point, the more useful question concerns value flow: which quantity is moving, where it came from, and what later stage depends upon it. Datapackets answer that question more directly than a rigid insistence on keeping every value attached to a register-shaped object.

The same principle shaped the treatment of control logic and multiplexers, much of which is centralised and resolved internally in the textbook datapath. In the prototype, multiplexers are not modelled as separate learner-facing components, and some control-facing consequences are distributed across the later phases of the lesson. That choice keeps Execute, Memory, Write-Back, and Program Counter Update educationally active, whereas a stricter concentration of all control into Decode would have left those later phases looking more like consequences to observe than decisions to understand.

Some hidden relationships were worth externalising because doing so made the logic easier to follow; others were better left in local panels, prompts, or background system behaviour because turning them into further embodied tasks would have added friction without much educational return. The result is not a full simulator of every internal detail, but a narrower representational model shaped around what a learner most needs in order to reconstruct the flow of execution coherently.

\section{Environment Structure and Lesson Flow}

\subsection{The World-Space Route}

Guided tracing required the lesson to unfold as a route rather than as a single viewing surface. The prototype was organised as a sequence of world-space stations through which the learner follows the active instruction, allowing the environment to control the pace at which each part of execution is introduced.

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{RoutedLessonMap.png}
\caption[Routed lesson map]{Annotated map of the VR environment showing the ordered route through the main lesson stations. The spatial sequence controls when each part of instruction execution becomes available to the learner and supports progression from one learner-facing phase to the next.}
\label{fig:routedLessonMap}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:routedLessonMap}, the routed map, directional cues, gate logic, and local landmarks all serve that purpose. They help the learner keep track of where they are in the execution path, what has already been resolved, and what remains to be decided. Traditional datapath diagrams can be powerful as overview material, but they present the entire system at once. In the prototype, the same system is encountered in a controlled sequence, which reduces the pressure to hold every relationship in mind at the same time.

\subsection{The Six Lesson Phases}

As Figure~\ref{fig:routedLessonMap} shows, the route itself is organised around six familiar lesson phases, isolated as parts of the reasoning chain: Fetch, Decode, Execute, Memory, Write-Back, and Program Counter Update. Fetch introduces the active instruction and establishes that execution begins from a definite source. Decode turns encoded structure into usable information by requiring the learner to identify fields, source operands, and immediate-related meaning where relevant. Execute gives architectural operations a visible decision point rather than leaving the ALU as a silent internal region in the diagram. The Memory phase clarifies the distinction between arithmetic behaviour and memory behaviour by giving addressing and data access their own local context. Write-Back provides a clear moment at which the learner can connect previous decisions with the final architectural update. Program Counter Update is separated for similar reasons: control-flow consequences are often harder to absorb when they remain implicit, so the design gives them a dedicated place in the lesson sequence. Across all six phases, the learner is not being asked to memorise a route through a virtual world for its own sake, but to reconstruct the flow of instruction execution through a series of linked decisions whose order becomes easier to follow once they are made spatial and local.

\section{Modes, Interaction, and Local Support}

\subsection{Modes as Variations on One Lesson}

As outlined in Section~4.2.2, the prototype uses Learning mode, Practice mode, and Test mode as variations on one lesson structure rather than as separate interpretations of the datapath. What changes across the modes is the level of support, tolerance, and learner responsibility.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{ModeFlowchart.png}
\caption[Three-mode lesson progression]{Pedagogical progression across Learning mode, Practice mode, and Test mode. The same lesson route is retained across all three modes while explanation, hints, tolerance, and other forms of support are progressively reduced so that the learner is asked to reconstruct more of the reasoning independently.}
\label{fig:modeProgression}
\end{figure}

Learning mode provides the fullest local explanation and therefore carries the strongest teaching load, helping the learner build an initial model of how an instruction moves through the datapath. Practice mode reduces that support while preserving enough scaffolding for the learner to attempt the route more deliberately. Test mode retains the same structure under stricter conditions and with much less help. This continuity matters because it lets the learner revisit the same reasoning under changing levels of support rather than confronting a different lesson each time.

\subsection{Physical Action and Local Guidance}

The interaction model follows the same selective logic already described in the representational choices. Physical action is used where it helps reveal structure: the learner moves through the route, handles the instruction, selects registers, works with datapackets, and completes local validation. Other forms of support remain in panels, prompts, and concise references because they are more useful there than they would be as further physical tasks. In practice, this allows the environment to remain active without becoming cumbersome.

Local guidance plays an important role in that balance. Panels, hints, and nearby prompts form part of the teaching strategy through which the route remains legible while the learner is still acquiring the structure of the lesson. The environment asks the learner to act where action clarifies the concept, but it does not ask the learner to carry every explanatory burden unaided at every moment.

\subsection{Validation and Conceptual Discipline}

The environment does not simply let the learner drift through the route and discover at the end that an early interpretation was wrong. Important decisions are checked near the point at which they are made so that an early misunderstanding does not propagate into later phases. Progress therefore depends on reasoning through the route coherently, not simply on reaching its final station.

\section{Core Design Decisions and Trade-Offs}

Several trade-offs define the final form of the prototype, the most important of which concerns pedagogical clarity and literal fidelity, a balance established earlier in the chapter. The remaining trade-offs concern breadth, interaction, and participant use.

Another trade-off concerns breadth and coherence. A wider system could have covered more instructions, more architectural topics, or a more detailed account of control and low-level circuitry, but that breadth would have weakened the central lesson loop and made it harder to defend the educational scope of the build.

There is also a trade-off between automation and learner action. If too much of the datapath remains automatic, the learner becomes a spectator. If too much is turned into interaction, the environment becomes cluttered and tiring. Much of the design work lay in deciding which decisions were worth preserving as learner action and which relationships were better carried by the environment itself. This balance also reflects a broader lesson from the literature: immersive space does not remove the need for restraint in information design \cite{korkut2023visualization,lee2020data}.

Finally, the design was shaped by the demands of participant use. This prototype does not have the freedom of an indefinitely expanding research platform. It had to become stable, navigable, and teachable within the available scope, which meant that features had to earn their place pedagogically and practically. Some ideas remained outside the final build not because they were uninteresting, but because they did not strengthen the educational core strongly enough to justify the complexity they would have introduced.

These trade-offs define the final design: a selective representational model shaped by pedagogical clarity, interaction burden, scope, and the practical demands of participant use.

\section{Summary}

This chapter defined the prototype as a supplementary learning environment built around a bounded set of learning outcomes for selected single-cycle MIPS datapath concepts. It established the decision to reorganise instruction execution into six learner-facing phases and showed how this shaped the routed environment, the progression across Learning mode, Practice mode, and Test mode, and the use of physical interaction, local guidance, and validation. The resulting design prioritises the reasoning structure needed by the learner while balancing architectural fidelity against clarity, interaction burden, and practical scope. Chapter~5 turns from these design decisions to their implementation in the prototype used for the participant study.
