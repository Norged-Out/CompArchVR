\chapter{Background and Related Work}

This chapter places the project in context. It begins with the educational difficulty posed by computer architecture, then considers what conventional teaching already does well and where some learners still struggle, before turning to interactive and immersive work in computer architecture and computer science education. It closes by identifying the design constraints and narrower gap from which the current prototype emerged.

\section{Computer Architecture as a Learning Problem}

Computer architecture asks students to reason about a machine whose most important behaviour is hidden. They must understand how an instruction is fetched, how its fields are decoded, how register values are selected, how the ALU is used for different instruction classes, how memory is addressed, and how the program counter is updated. None of this is beyond explanation on paper, but the subject places a particular burden on the learner because several representations have to be held together at once. A datapath diagram shows structure, an instruction format shows encoding, an assembly statement shows intent, and a trace shows outcome. The difficulty lies in turning those partial views into one connected account of execution.

Porter et al. developed concept questions for computer architecture courses and found that students were often ``not learning as much as the instructors expected,'' with a per-question average of only 56\% across the studied cohorts \cite{porter2013evaluating}. Their account is especially useful because it separates procedural success from conceptual understanding. Students may learn how to complete familiar exercises and still, in their phrase, miss ``the proverbial forest for the trees'' \cite{porter2013evaluating}. In architecture teaching, that distinction is important. A learner may be able to label datapath components, fill in a pipeline table, or work through a cache example while still struggling to explain what the processor is doing at a given moment.

Work on architecture visualization points in much the same direction. Yehezkel et al. argue that students using conventional tools can end up treating the machine too much like a black box, whereas a stronger visual environment can help them construct ``a viable mental model of the computer'' \cite{yehezkel2007contribution}. The problem, then, is not simply that architecture contains difficult facts. The harder task is to connect assembly syntax, instruction fields, datapath structure, register state, memory state, and control behaviour over time into a single explanation of execution.

The work reported here concentrates on instruction execution in a single-cycle MIPS datapath, a limited but still revealing part of this wider area. A learner still has to decode the instruction, identify source and destination registers, select operands, follow value flow through the datapath, and predict the resulting architectural state.

\section{Conventional Teaching and Its Limits}

None of this implies that conventional teaching is ineffective. In most programmes, computer architecture is taught progressively rather than all at once. Basic logic comes first, then gates, then combinations such as half adders and full adders, then larger datapath components, and only after that does the learner meet the processor as an integrated system. Official module descriptions reflect that build-up directly through sequences that move from digital logic and register transfer into datapath design and the fetch-decode-execute cycle \cite{tcdComputerArchitectureI}. That staged development matters, and nothing in this dissertation argues against it.

Traditional tools also do several things very well. Lectures and tutorials provide order. Whiteboard derivations slow a problem down and show how one step follows from the next. Static datapath diagrams are strong at showing structure, naming components, and fixing the shape of the machine in the learner's mind. Simulators and assembly tools are valuable for correctness, inspection, and repeated practice because they let students test programs, inspect registers and memory, and connect assembly syntax to machine behaviour in a more formal way than a verbal explanation alone.

A learner cannot make sensible use of the VR environment without some prior exposure to the underlying architectural ideas. The prototype was never intended to replace the gradual conceptual build-up provided by lectures, diagrams, exercises, and conventional tools. It sits alongside those materials at the point where a student has already seen the structure of the datapath but still struggles to turn that structure into a working mental model of execution flow.

Fukuda et al. frame a related problem in systems teaching as a balance between ``extreme abstract'' learning and ``extreme technical'' learning \cite{fukuda2020practical}, a distinction that is useful here because it suggests that observation alone may leave students with too little experience and too little comprehensive understanding. The same distinction helps define the present problem. Conventional teaching can still leave some learners needing additional help at the point where observed behaviour has to be turned into a connected explanation.

The limitation of conventional teaching is therefore narrower than a claim that the tools do not work. Some of the most useful traditional materials are strongest either at showing structure or at checking correctness. They may be less helpful during first-pass mental-model building for learners who need to see how information moves through the machine as a sequence of linked decisions. A datapath diagram can show where the ALU sits, and a simulator can show that a register changed, but for some students the connection between those two facts is not immediate. A spatial tool is attractive precisely in that gap.

\section{Interactive Support for Computer Architecture Learning}

Before turning to VR, it is worth noting that architecture learning has already been explored through other interactive formats, including an educational mobile game for computer architecture in which learners found the approach motivating and were willing to use it again \cite{tlili2016improving}. Even without immersion, the study still matters because it suggests that the subject responds well to interactive presentation and because it keeps attention on the deeper issue of making abstract system behaviour easier to follow.

More directly related work appears in immersive and experiential systems. One early VR environment for studying computer architecture through experiential learning asked learners to assemble virtual computer components while receiving textual guidance \cite{dascalu2017experiential}. The emphasis differs from the current project, yet the paper remains important because it treats computer architecture as a credible target for immersive learning rather than as a novelty application. It also represents an early attempt to move architecture teaching away from passive explanation and toward structured activity inside a virtual environment.

The paper closest in spirit to this project is the VR serious game described by Tan et al. \cite{tan2023vr}, whose authors describe Computer Organisation and Architecture as ``dry, tough, and complex'' and argue that students struggle because they cannot ``visualize the procedures of various CPU functions'' \cite{tan2023vr}. Their system is organised around tutorials, self-guided assignments, and quizzes, and it covers topics such as fetching and executing instructions, interrupt handling, interrupt-driven I/O, and context switches. Help material is provided in a ``step-by-step manner'' before that support is reduced in later tasks \cite{tan2023vr}. The relevance of that work lies not only in its use of VR, but in the way it turns architecture learning into a guided sequence of actions rather than a static visual reference, even though the prototype developed here narrows its scope more tightly around single-cycle datapath execution.

Checa et al. provide a nearby precedent from computer-hardware learning rather than architecture proper \cite{checa2023immersive}. Their VR serious game is about hardware assembly, not datapath tracing, but the study suggests that immersive learning may be more helpful for understanding, visual recognition, and making connections between concepts than for simple factual recall \cite{checa2023immersive}. That distinction matters here. The aim is not primarily to help students memorise terminology, but to help them connect instruction fields, datapath structure, and resulting machine state into one explanation.

A recent VR environment for digital design and computer organisation laboratory learning is also relevant for a simpler reason: it keeps usability in view throughout the evaluation \cite{alnuaimi2025vr}. Even a promising educational VR system has to be learnable and comfortable enough to use before any learning claim can matter. A tool intended to support understanding of hidden processes can just as easily create another layer of confusion if the interface is awkward or overloaded.

\section{Virtual Reality in Computer Science Education}

The broader computer science education literature gives a clearer sense of where architecture sits within immersive teaching work. Reviews of VR in computer science education note its promise in areas where abstract concepts benefit from interactive visualisation and show that many applications are directed toward conceptual learning rather than toward full practical programming tasks \cite{pirker2020virtual,agbo2021application,janani2026virtual}. Janani et al. are especially useful because they treat VR as strongest for abstract and process-oriented topics while also describing it as a complementary teaching medium \cite{janani2026virtual}.

One comparison of VR and desktop learning for sorting algorithms reports stronger subjective measures such as presence, absorption, and positive emotion in the VR condition \cite{pirker2021potential}. A mobile VR application for learning Python also reports encouraging results, but frames VR more as a useful aid than as a complete substitute for ordinary teaching \cite{mohamad2023immersive}. Dengel reaches a similarly cautious conclusion from another angle, arguing that immersive environments can support learning in computer science without implying that higher immersion will automatically yield better learning outcomes in every case \cite{dengel2020important}. Across these studies, the same pattern appears: VR is often most plausible as a way of making a first encounter with a difficult idea more concrete.

The same reviews also point to the limits of the field. Recurring usability issues, weak collaborative features, and a lack of clear instructional grounding appear across parts of the literature, and the work remains scattered across topics and methods \cite{janani2026virtual,agbo2021application}. A useful contribution, then, does not depend on claiming that VR in computer science education is entirely new; it can instead take the form of a careful application in a domain where the match between problem and medium is especially strong.

\section{Design Constraints and Transferable Lessons}

A case for VR still has to be carried by the quality of the design itself. The harder question is what kind of VR design is likely to help rather than hinder. Work on educational VR discusses practical constraints as much as opportunity and draws attention to issues such as deployment, accessibility, sustainability, and the amount of support institutions need before VR becomes a normal teaching tool \cite{cook2019challenges}. The emphasis therefore shifts away from spectacle and toward a more modest question: can a system be clear, usable, and worth the time required to use it?

A related point appears in work on VR visualisation, which shows that immersive space does not automatically solve information-design problems \cite{korkut2023visualization}. Poor layout, visual clutter, and badly organised interaction can still make a system hard to read. That lesson carries directly into architecture teaching, where the central challenge is already one of representing a hidden process without losing the learner in detail: if too much of the datapath is surfaced at once, VR may simply reproduce the original problem in another form.

A broader review of VR in education reaches a similar conclusion by showing continuing interest in the medium alongside repeated concerns about readiness, hardware access, discomfort, and the quality of actual learning design \cite{samala2025virtual}. Lee et al. make a different but related argument: immersive experience can help restore intuitive understanding where abstraction has flattened away the underlying reality \cite{lee2020data}. Although their work is not about architecture, the idea transfers well: a datapath diagram is useful precisely because it abstracts, yet that abstraction can become too detached from the felt sequence of execution. The central design problem is therefore not how to make the learner wander through a visually impressive machine, but how to make the essential steps of execution more graspable without adding another layer of clutter.

\section{Implications for Design}

The literature points toward a fairly specific use of VR in computer architecture. It also suggests that the most useful systems are selective in what they externalise, what they keep simple, and where they ask the learner to act rather than only observe.

Architecture learning also appears to benefit from guidance and progression. The architecture-facing papers repeatedly use tutorials, staged tasks, and support material before learners are expected to work more independently \cite{tlili2016improving,tan2023vr}. Similar patterns appear in the broader computer science education literature, where prompts, panels, and gradual reduction of support are common design choices \cite{janani2026virtual}.

The literature also warns repeatedly against overload. A datapath can be made more visible in VR, but that does not mean every hidden detail should be turned into an explicit interaction. If the learner is asked to manage too many objects, too much text, or too much movement at once, much of the value of the medium quickly disappears \cite{cook2019challenges,korkut2023visualization,samala2025virtual,lee2020data}.

Against that background, the gap addressed here can be stated fairly plainly. There is already evidence that interactive and immersive approaches can support computer architecture learning, and there is a broader body of work showing that VR can help with abstract computer science concepts. Less well served is a tightly scoped learning environment centred on manual instruction tracing through a single-cycle datapath, with explicit attention to operand selection, stage-by-stage decisions, and the final architectural state. That is the space occupied by the current prototype. The next chapter turns from the literature to the method used to evaluate it.

\section{Summary}

The literature supports a narrower claim than any simple assertion that VR is automatically good for education, or automatically good for computer architecture. Existing work shows that architecture learning has already been explored through mobile games, experiential environments, and VR serious games, while the wider VR-in-computer-science-education literature suggests that conceptual learning is one of the medium's more plausible uses. The same literature also sets clear limits: guidance matters, usability matters, and information has to be presented with restraint. Those conditions shape the prototype evaluated in the next chapter.
