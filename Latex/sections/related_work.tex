\chapter{Background and Related Work}

This chapter establishes the context by examining the learning problem itself, then reviewing interactive and immersive work in computer architecture and computer science education, and finally drawing out the constraints and gaps that shaped the project.

\section{Computer Architecture as a Learning Problem}

Computer architecture asks students to reason about a machine whose most important behaviour is hidden. They are expected to understand how an instruction is accessed, how instruction fields are decoded, how register values are selected, how the ALU is used for different classes of instruction, how memory is addressed, and how the program counter is updated. None of this is impossible to explain on paper, but it does place a heavy burden on the learner because the subject requires them to move back and forth between several representations at once. Static diagrams can show structure, and short traces can show examples, but the harder step is often understanding how a labelled picture becomes a moving process.

That difficulty is not just anecdotal. Porter et al. developed concept questions for computer architecture courses and found that students were often ``not learning as much as the instructors expected,'' with a per-question average of only 56\% across the studied cohorts \cite{porter2013evaluating}. Their account is especially useful here because it distinguishes between procedural success and conceptual understanding; students may learn how to complete standard exercises and still, in their phrase, miss ``the proverbial forest for the trees'' \cite{porter2013evaluating}. That observation fits architecture teaching well: a learner may be able to label datapath components, fill out a pipeline diagram, or work through a cache example while still struggling to explain what the machine is actually doing.

Work on architecture visualization points in the same direction. Yehezkel et al. argue that students using conventional tools can end up treating the machine too much like a black box, whereas a stronger visual environment can help them construct ``a viable mental model of the computer'' \cite{yehezkel2007contribution}. The issue, then, is not simply that architecture contains difficult facts, but that understanding depends on linking assembly syntax, instruction fields, datapath structure, register state, memory state, and control behaviour over time into one coherent account of execution.

This dissertation focuses on a deliberately limited part of this wider area: instruction execution in a single-cycle MIPS datapath. That scope does not cover all of architecture, but it is broad enough to expose the central difficulties at issue here. A learner still has to decode the instruction, identify source and destination registers, select operands, follow value flow through the datapath, and predict the resulting architectural state.

\section{Conventional Teaching and Its Limits}

None of this means that conventional teaching is ineffective. In most programmes, computer architecture is taught progressively rather than all at once. Basic logic is introduced first, then gates, then combinations such as half adders and full adders, then larger datapath components, and only after that does the learner meet the processor as an integrated system. Official module descriptions reflect that build-up directly through sequences that move from digital logic and register transfer into datapath design and the fetch-decode-execute cycle \cite{tcdComputerArchitectureI}. That kind of staged development matters, and the present dissertation does not argue against it.

Traditional tools also do several things very well. Lectures and tutorials provide a clear order in which ideas can be introduced. Whiteboard derivations are useful for slowing a problem down and showing how each step follows from the previous one. Static datapath diagrams are strong at showing structure, naming components, and fixing the shape of the machine in the learner's mind. Simulators and assembly tools, meanwhile, are valuable for correctness, inspection, and repeated practice because they let students test programs, inspect registers and memory, and connect assembly syntax to machine behaviour in a more formal way than a verbal explanation alone.

These strengths matter because the project depends on them. A learner cannot make sensible use of the VR environment without some prior exposure to the underlying architectural ideas. The prototype is therefore not intended to replace the gradual conceptual build-up provided by lectures, diagrams, exercises, and conventional tools. It is meant to sit alongside them, especially at the point where a student has seen the structure of the datapath but still struggles to turn that structure into a working mental model of execution flow.

Fukuda et al. frame a related problem in systems teaching as a balance between ``extreme abstract'' learning and ``extreme technical'' learning \cite{fukuda2020practical}. Their point is not that visualization is useless, but that observation alone may leave students with too little experience and too little comprehensive understanding. The same point helps here. Conventional teaching is not weak because it lacks content; it becomes less effective at a narrower point where some learners still need help turning observed behaviour into a connected explanation.

The limitation of conventional teaching is therefore narrower than a simple claim that the tools do not work. Some of the most useful traditional materials are strongest either at showing structure or at checking correctness. They are sometimes weaker at first-pass mental-model building for learners who need to see how information moves through the machine as a sequence of linked decisions. A datapath diagram can show where the ALU sits, and a simulator can show that a register changed, but for some students the connection between those two facts is not immediate. It is in that gap that a supplementary spatial tool may be useful.

\section{Interactive Support for Computer Architecture Learning}

Before focusing on VR, it is worth noting that architecture learning has already been explored through other interactive formats. An educational mobile game for computer architecture reported that learners found the approach motivating and were willing to use it again \cite{tlili2016improving}. That work is not immersive, but it still matters for two reasons: it suggests that the subject does respond well to interactive presentation, and it shows that the deeper issue is not VR itself so much as how to make abstract system behaviour easier to follow.

More directly related work appears in immersive and experiential systems. One early VR environment for studying computer architecture through experiential learning asked learners to assemble virtual computer components while receiving textual guidance \cite{dascalu2017experiential}. The emphasis is not identical to the present project, but its importance lies in treating computer architecture as a credible target for immersive learning rather than as a niche or novelty application. Just as importantly, it shows an early attempt to move architecture teaching away from passive explanation and toward structured activity inside a virtual environment.

The paper closest in spirit to the present project is the VR serious game described by Tan et al. \cite{tan2023vr}. The authors describe Computer Organisation and Architecture as ``dry, tough, and complex'' and argue that students struggle because they cannot ``visualize the procedures of various CPU functions'' \cite{tan2023vr}. Their system is organised around tutorials, self-guided assignments, and quizzes, and it covers topics such as fetching and executing instructions, interrupt handling, interrupt-driven I/O, and context switches. They also explicitly provide help material in a ``step-by-step manner'' before removing that support in later tasks \cite{tan2023vr}. Their work shows a serious attempt to turn architecture learning into a guided sequence of actions rather than a static visual reference, even if the current project narrows its scope more tightly around single-cycle datapath execution.

Checa et al. provide a nearby precedent from computer-hardware learning rather than architecture proper \cite{checa2023immersive}. Their VR serious game is about hardware assembly, not datapath tracing, but the study suggests that immersive learning may be more helpful for understanding, visual recognition, and making connections between concepts than for simple factual recall \cite{checa2023immersive}. That emphasis fits this project well. The aim here is not primarily to help students memorise terminology, but to help them connect instruction fields, datapath structure, and resulting machine state into one explanation.

A recent VR environment for digital design and computer organisation laboratory learning is also relevant here for a simpler reason: it keeps usability in view throughout the evaluation \cite{alnuaimi2025vr}. Its results point to a familiar lesson. Even a promising educational VR system has to be learnable and comfortable enough to use before any learning claim can matter. A tool intended to support understanding of hidden processes can just as easily create another layer of confusion if the interface is awkward or overloaded.

\section{Virtual Reality in Computer Science Education}

The broader computer science education literature gives a clearer sense of where architecture fits within immersive teaching work. Reviews of VR in computer science education note its promise for areas where abstract concepts benefit from interactive visualisation and show that many applications are directed toward conceptual learning rather than toward full practical programming tasks \cite{pirker2020virtual,agbo2021application,janani2026virtual}. Janani et al. are particularly useful here because they treat VR as strongest for abstract and process-oriented topics while also describing it as a complementary teaching medium rather than a full replacement \cite{janani2026virtual}.

This pattern fits the current project well. The aim here is to support conceptual understanding of instruction execution, not to teach the whole of digital logic design inside a headset. The wider reviews suggest that this is exactly the sort of problem for which VR is most often proposed: an abstract process, difficult to observe directly, that may become easier to follow when it is made spatial, interactive, and staged.

One comparison of VR and desktop learning for sorting algorithms reports stronger subjective measures such as presence, absorption, and positive emotion in the VR condition \cite{pirker2021potential}. A mobile VR application for learning Python also reports encouraging results, but frames VR more as a useful aid than as a complete substitute for ordinary teaching \cite{mohamad2023immersive}. Dengel reaches a similarly cautious conclusion from another angle, arguing that immersive environments can support learning in computer science without implying that higher immersion will automatically yield better learning outcomes in every case \cite{dengel2020important}. Taken together, these studies suggest a recurring pattern in the literature: VR is often most plausible as a way of making a first encounter with a difficult idea more concrete, not as a universal replacement for existing formal tools.

The same reviews also point to the limits of the field. Recurring usability issues, weak collaborative features, and a lack of clear instructional grounding appear across parts of the literature, and the work remains scattered across topics and methods \cite{janani2026virtual,agbo2021application}. This matters because it suggests that a worthwhile contribution does not need to claim that VR in CS education is entirely new. A more realistic aim is to show a careful application in a domain where the match between problem and medium is strong.

\section{Design Constraints and Transferable Lessons}

The case for VR is therefore not enough on its own. The next question is what kind of VR design is likely to help rather than hinder. Work on educational VR discusses practical constraints as much as opportunity and draws attention to issues such as deployment, accessibility, sustainability, and the amount of support institutions need before VR becomes a normal teaching tool \cite{cook2019challenges}. This shifts the emphasis away from spectacle and toward a more modest question: can a system be clear, usable, and worth the time required to use it?

A related point appears in work on VR visualisation, which shows that immersive space does not automatically solve information-design problems \cite{korkut2023visualization}. Poor layout, visual clutter, and badly organised interaction can still make a system hard to read. That lesson carries directly into architecture teaching, where the central challenge is already one of representing a hidden process without losing the learner in detail. If too much of the datapath is surfaced at once, VR may simply reproduce the original problem in another form.

A broader review of VR in education makes a similar point by showing continuing interest in the medium alongside repeated concerns about readiness, hardware access, discomfort, and the quality of actual learning design \cite{samala2025virtual}. Lee et al. make a different but related argument: immersive experience can help restore intuitive understanding where abstraction has flattened away the underlying reality \cite{lee2020data}. Their work is not about architecture, but the idea transfers well. A datapath diagram is useful precisely because it abstracts; the risk is that the abstraction becomes too detached from the felt sequence of execution. The design problem, then, is not how to make the learner wander through a visually impressive machine, but how to make the essential steps of execution more graspable without adding another layer of clutter.

\section{Implications for the Present Project}

Several design implications follow from this literature. First, the case for using VR in computer architecture does not rest on novelty, but on a specific match between medium and problem: hidden, dynamic behaviour that learners often struggle to reconstruct from a static diagram. Second, the most useful systems are not simply more immersive versions of existing teaching material. They are selective in what they externalise, what they keep simple, and where they ask the learner to act rather than only observe.

Architecture learning also seems to benefit from guidance and progression. This appears directly in the architecture-facing papers, where tutorials, staged tasks, and support material are repeatedly used before learners are expected to work more independently \cite{tlili2016improving,tan2023vr}. It also appears in the broader CS-education literature, where prompts, panels, and gradual reduction of support are common design patterns \cite{janani2026virtual}. Taken together, these patterns support the use of Learning, Practice, and Test as a progression rather than as three unrelated modes.

The literature also repeatedly warns against overload. A datapath can be made more visible in VR, but that does not mean every hidden detail should be turned into an explicit interaction. Several sources point in the same direction: if the learner is asked to manage too many objects, too much text, or too much movement at once, the value of the medium quickly starts to disappear \cite{cook2019challenges,korkut2023visualization,samala2025virtual,lee2020data}.

The gap addressed by the present project can therefore be stated fairly plainly. There is already evidence that interactive and immersive approaches can support computer architecture learning, and there is a broader body of work showing that VR can help with abstract computer science concepts. What remains less well served is a tightly scoped learning environment centred on manual instruction tracing through a single-cycle datapath, with explicit attention to operand selection, stage-by-stage decisions, and the final architectural state. That is the niche occupied by the current prototype, and the next chapter explains how its design was shaped by both the literature reviewed here and the teaching problem set out in Chapter 1.

\section{Summary}

The literature does not justify an argument that VR is automatically good for education, or even automatically good for computer architecture. What it does justify is a narrower claim: computer architecture is a strong candidate for interactive support because much of the subject depends on understanding dynamic processes that are hard to see directly. Existing work shows that architecture learning has already been explored through mobile games, experiential environments, and VR serious games, while the wider VR-in-CS-education literature suggests that conceptual learning is one of the medium's more plausible uses. At the same time, that literature also sets clear limits. Guidance matters, usability matters, and information must be presented with restraint. Those are the conditions under which the present dissertation takes shape.
