\chapter{Methodology}

This chapter sets out the method used to evaluate the prototype developed in this project. The study was conceived as a small-scale exploratory evaluation of a deliberately scoped educational prototype, not as a large controlled experiment intended to prove that virtual reality should replace conventional teaching methods.

\section{Research Framing}

The study asked whether the developed prototype appeared educationally useful within its intended scope, whether participants could engage with it clearly enough for that educational value to matter, and whether the collected evidence supported the view that the environment showed promise for learning selected computer architecture concepts.

As noted in Chapter 1, the prototype grew out of repeated teaching-assistant experience in computer organisation. The study therefore examined whether the guided tracing that proved useful in that setting could be translated into a useful VR-based learning environment, a question that also sits in line with earlier architecture-focused immersive work \cite{dascalu2017experiential,tan2023vr}.

\subsection{Research Question}

The dissertation was guided by the following research question:

\begin{quote}
\textit{To what extent can an immersive virtual reality learning environment support students' understanding of selected single-cycle MIPS datapath concepts as a supplementary educational tool?}
\end{quote}

In this dissertation, the phrase ``to what extent'' is to be interpreted in a bounded exploratory sense rather than as a promise of a full classroom control-group comparison. More specifically, the question is answered through three linked forms of evidence: participant perceptions of the environment, descriptive concept evidence drawn from the baseline subset and post-session responses, and observed progression or support needs across the session structure.

The purpose of the study was therefore to determine whether this particular prototype, used in this particular educational context, offered enough conceptual clarity, usability, and perceived value to justify the design choices behind it and support later work on broader evaluation.

\subsection{Hypotheses}

On that basis, the study was organised around one main hypothesis and a small set of bounded supporting hypotheses that guided the evaluation.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Main hypothesis.} A structured immersive virtual reality learning environment that externalises instruction execution into visible, spatial, and interactive steps can support students' understanding of selected single-cycle MIPS datapath concepts and be perceived as a useful supplementary educational tool for teaching computer architecture.
\end{quote}

The supporting hypotheses are as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{H1: Usability and clarity.} Participants would generally report that the system was understandable, navigable, and usable after a short introduction.
\item \textbf{H2: Conceptual support.} Participants would report that the environment helped them understand instruction flow, datapath phases, and component roles clearly based on the interactions.
\item \textbf{H3: Guided-to-independent progression.} After guided exposure in Learning mode, participants would be better able to complete tasks with reduced support in Practice or Test mode.
\item \textbf{H4: Supplementary value.} Participants would tend to describe the system as a helpful supplementary learning tool rather than as a replacement for lectures, diagrams, or other conventional teaching materials.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Alignment with the Learning Outcomes}

The evaluation was aligned with the learning outcomes defined for the prototype, but those outcomes belong primarily to the design of the system and are discussed in full in Chapter 4. In methodological terms, they matter here because they establish what the study should look for and what kinds of claims the evidence can reasonably support.

The study therefore focused on a bounded cluster of educational tasks: decoding supported instructions, explaining the roles of major datapath components, tracing instruction and value flow across the main lesson phases, making the required operand and control-facing choices, predicting resulting architectural state changes, comparing instruction classes, and completing the walkthrough with progressively less support. These categories were reflected across the baseline subset, the post-session knowledge check, the observation notes, and the broader interpretation of participant feedback.


\section{Study Design and Rationale}

The study is best described as a small-scale exploratory participant evaluation of a focused educational prototype. Structured evidence came from the background form, the baseline subset, the Likert-style post-session items addressing perceptions of the system, and the knowledge check on selected concepts. Open-ended evidence came from written participant feedback and from researcher observation notes recorded during and after the session. Considered together, these sources were intended to provide a fuller picture of how the prototype functioned as a learning tool.

The evaluation remained exploratory rather than classroom-trial in scale. The participant pool was limited, there was no formal non-VR control group, and the study did not attempt to measure long-term retention. Its purpose was to examine how participants engaged with the prototype, what kinds of understanding or difficulty became visible during use, how participants described the experience afterward, and whether those strands of evidence broadly supported the same interpretation.

The rationale for this design followed directly from the prototype's educational role as a supplementary intervention at the point of difficulty identified earlier in the dissertation. The short refresher before the session, the mode structure, and the post-session evaluation all reflected that supplementary role. This position is consistent with broader literature on conceptual VR support in computer science education \cite{janani2026virtual,dengel2020important}. The study therefore asked whether a guided, spatial, learner-facing environment could make a difficult representational problem easier to grasp.

\section{Participants}

Participants were recruited through voluntary convenience sampling. The approved study design targeted consenting adult participants aged 18 or over who were willing to take part in an evaluation of the VR learning environment and able to provide informed consent. The ethics submission set a target sample size of twenty participants, which was judged sufficient for identifying patterns in conceptual understanding, usability, and participant feedback within the practical limits of the project. No payment was offered for participation. Individuals who experienced severe discomfort or motion sickness with VR could opt out.

Recruitment took place by email invitation, with interested individuals contacting the researcher directly before any consent was obtained. The recruitment materials described a single in-person study session and made clear that participation was voluntary and that withdrawal was permitted at any time without penalty. The ethics submission also records that there was no dependent relationship between the research team and the participants, which reduced the risk of coercion and allowed the responses to be read with greater confidence. Because the project was not restricted to one narrow classroom cohort, participants were expected to vary in prior architecture exposure, prior headset use, and confidence with the topic. That variation was captured explicitly through the background section of the questionnaire so that later findings could be interpreted in light of where each participant was starting from.

\section{Procedure}

Each participant session followed the same broad structure: consent and background capture, a short baseline probe, a tailored refresher, the VR session itself, and a post-session evaluation.

The session began with the Participant Information Leaflet and written informed consent, both of which are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:pil} and Appendix~\ref{app:consent} respectively. Participants were given the opportunity to read the study information, ask questions, and decide whether they wished to continue before any data collection began. They then completed the background section of the questionnaire, which captured age confirmation, prior VR use, prior exposure to computer architecture or computer organisation, self-reported familiarity and confidence, and the kinds of architecture topics they usually found difficult.

After the background section, participants were offered a short baseline subset drawn from the knowledge check. This consisted of five pre-session questions used as a modest checkpoint on foundational ideas before the VR experience began, specifically Questions 11--14 and 25 from the post-session questionnaire. Because this baseline acted as a quick pre-session scan rather than a mandatory scored gate, unanswered items were retained as missing rather than treated as incorrect. Participants then received a short verbal refresher tailored to their prior experience. The refresher was intentionally high-level: it was not meant to teach the whole content in advance, but to align terminology, reduce avoidable confusion, and give participants enough orientation to engage meaningfully with the prototype. Typical coverage included the basic form of an assembly instruction, what the datapath represents, what the main instruction stages do, how registers and memory differ, where control choices matter, and what kinds of interaction participants would encounter in the VR environment.

Participants then completed the VR session using selected instructions and one or more of the available modes. A recorded walkthrough of the prototype, representative of what participants were seeing during the live study, is available at \url{https://youtu.be/Y8tFYnWeLtI}. The exact path depended on participant background, session time, and whether the participant appeared comfortable enough to move into reduced-guidance modes. A typical path included one or two Learning mode instructions, one Practice mode instruction, and, where appropriate, a further Test mode attempt. During the session, the researcher intervened only when needed, and participants were encouraged to make full use of the resources available inside the environment. The amount and kind of intervention required were themselves treated as meaningful observational data.

After removing the headset, participants completed the remaining sections of the questionnaire. These included Likert-style ratings on usability, engagement, and perceived learning, followed by the broader knowledge-check section and open-ended feedback. Alongside the formal questionnaire data, the researcher maintained a separate participant notes file recording the refresher actually delivered, the instruction and mode path attempted, points of hesitation, the level of support required, and any explicit comparisons participants made with traditional materials.

\section{Instruments and Data Capture}

The study used several complementary instruments, each serving a different purpose. The primary structured instrument was the Microsoft Forms questionnaire used in the live study. Its opening section captured background context, including prior architecture exposure, prior VR use, self-reported confidence, and prior topic familiarity. The system-experience sections then gathered Likert-style responses on usability, engagement, and perceived learning. After that, the form presented a targeted knowledge check, a follow-up confidence item relating to that section, and a final set of open-ended feedback questions. A reconstructed print version of the deployed questionnaire is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:questionnaire}. The Participant Information Leaflet, consent form, and representative recruitment materials are reproduced separately in Appendix~\ref{app:pil}, Appendix~\ref{app:consent}, and Appendix~\ref{app:recruitment}.

The knowledge-check portion of the live form focused mainly on topics relevant to the implemented prototype: program-counter behaviour, fetch, the instruction register, stage order, the execute stage, branch-related reasoning, and the behaviour of supported MIPS instructions such as \texttt{add}, \texttt{addi}, \texttt{lw}, \texttt{sw}, \texttt{beq}, and \texttt{j}. A small number of surrounding architecture concepts were also retained so that responses could be interpreted against the broader background knowledge with which participants entered the session, while the main evaluative focus remained on the selected datapath concepts supported by the prototype.

The baseline probe drew on the same five foundational concepts already preserved in the formal knowledge check, namely Questions 11--14 and 25. This gave the study a limited pre-session comparison point while keeping the evaluation aligned with the live instrument and the actual session flow.

Researcher notes were used to record session metadata on attempted instructions and modes, and to capture what the questionnaire alone could not record well: where participants hesitated, which concepts caused confusion, whether they could move from Learning mode to reduced-support modes, whether support was needed for navigation or controls, and how they positioned the experience relative to diagrams, lectures, or simulators.

\section{Analysis Plan}

The analysis addresses a practical question: what does the collected evidence suggest about usability, conceptual support, participant understanding, and the value of the prototype as a supplementary learning tool? The goal is not to force strong inferential conclusions out of a small participant study. It is to build a coherent account from several modest sources of evidence and then interpret that account honestly. IBM SPSS Statistics and NVivo were selected as the principal tools for quantitative and qualitative analysis respectively.

On the quantitative side, the analysis remains mainly descriptive. The background section can be used to summarise prior VR experience, prior architecture exposure, confidence, and reported areas of difficulty across the participant group. The Likert-style sections can be reported through item frequencies and, where useful, means or medians. Knowledge-check performance can be summarised through total scores, average performance, and identification of the most frequently missed questions. The post-knowledge-check confidence item can also be used as a small supporting indicator when discussing how secure participants felt about their answers. Where the short baseline subset was used, those pre-session responses can be compared descriptively with related post-session patterns to indicate whether targeted concepts appeared to have become clearer after the intervention.

On the qualitative side, open-ended responses and researcher observation notes are read together through a light thematic analysis. Likely themes include what helped understanding, what remained confusing, where the interface or navigation created friction, how the guidance structure was experienced, and how participants compared the prototype with more conventional learning materials.

Because the study does not include a formal non-VR control group, comparison is handled indirectly. One route is participant perception of the system relative to prior learning materials. A second is the limited baseline-versus-post-session comparison on selected concept questions. A third is observed progression from guided to less-guided performance during the session itself. Because the baseline subset was followed by a short verbal refresher before the VR session itself, any baseline/post-session differences should be interpreted as performance following the overall learning session rather than as evidence attributable to the VR environment alone.

\section{Ethical Considerations and Study Boundaries}

The study was conducted under School research ethics approval, and participation was entirely voluntary. Before taking part, each participant was provided with a Participant Information Leaflet explaining the purpose of the research, the structure of the session, the kinds of data being collected, and the intended use of the results. Written consent was obtained before the session began, and participants were given the opportunity to ask questions before deciding whether to continue. They were also informed that they could withdraw at any time without penalty and that, once research data had been anonymised and incorporated into the analysis, later withdrawal of that material might no longer be possible.

The principal participant risk was temporary discomfort associated with VR use, including eye strain, dizziness, or motion sickness. For that reason, the session design placed participant welfare ahead of task completion. Participants were informed in advance that they could pause or stop the session immediately if they felt unwell, and the headset could be removed without delay if any discomfort arose. The study therefore treated safe participation as a prerequisite for data collection rather than as a secondary practical concern.

Data protection was handled by separating administrative information from the research data itself. Any contact details used for recruitment or scheduling were stored separately from participant responses, and research materials were anonymised as early as practicable for analysis and reporting. Access to identifiable data was restricted to what was necessary for the conduct of the study, and storage was limited to password-protected and university-approved systems in line with the approved data-handling plan. Identifiable or coded administrative data were retained only for a limited period before deletion.

The study also used a Meta VR headset supplied through the School rather than requiring participants to use personal accounts or personal devices. Participants were not asked to sign into their own Meta accounts, and the research team did not intentionally collect Meta account data. Even so, the participant materials made clear that, because third-party hardware and software were involved, limited device-level or platform-level processing outside the direct control of the research team could still occur.

The evaluation was small in scale and exploratory in character. Its design does not support strong claims about long-term retention, broad generalisability, or causal proof that virtual reality is superior to conventional teaching, and the prototype itself remains a supplement to lectures, diagrams, tutorials, and other standard forms of instruction. The claims supported by the study are therefore narrower and more appropriate to the evidence: that a carefully scoped VR environment may help make the targeted computer architecture concepts more tangible, and that participant responses, post-session concept evidence, and observed progression can be used to judge whether such an approach appears promising as a supplementary educational tool.
