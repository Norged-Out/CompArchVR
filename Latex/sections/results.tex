\chapter{Results}

This chapter reports the findings from the participant study described in Chapter~3. The results are organised around five strands of evidence: participant background, the post-session questionnaire, post-session knowledge-check performance, the repeated baseline/post-session comparison, and qualitative material drawn from the open-ended questionnaire responses and researcher session notes. The full participant questionnaire is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:questionnaire}. Interpretation of these findings in relation to the research question and the literature reviewed in Chapter~2 is reserved for Chapter~7.

\section{Baseline and Post-Session Comparison}

The baseline probe was administered before both the tailored refresher and the VR session, following the procedure set out in Chapter~3. It repeated five items from the later knowledge check: Q11, Q12, Q13, Q14, and Q25. The repeated set provided a limited comparison on the same foundational concepts at two points in the study procedure. Since the tailored refresher occurred after the baseline probe and before the VR session, the comparison reported in this section describes change across the overall learning session rather than change attributable to the VR environment alone.

Some participants left individual baseline items unanswered, and missing responses were retained as missing rather than scored as incorrect. The resulting matched sample sizes were 11 for Q11, 11 for Q12, 10 for Q13, nine for Q14, and 11 for Q25. Eight participants answered all five baseline items and therefore contributed to the strict complete-case comparison. \section{Qualitative Results}

The final five questionnaire questions invited open-ended comments on what helped understanding, what remained confusing, what participants would change, whether the interface interfered with learning, and whether they would use the system again. These responses were considered alongside the anonymised researcher notes recorded during the sessions. The qualitative material was grouped into a small set of recurring categories, while participant comments and researcher observations were kept distinguishable because they represent different forms of evidence.

\subsection{Open-Ended Questionnaire Feedback}

Two forms of support appeared repeatedly in responses to the question asking what helped understanding most: direct interaction with the represented architecture and the local explanatory material provided around the lesson. One participant referred specifically to ``the hands-on interactions such as picking up the registers and loading them'', while another described ``the act of doing things'' as the aspect that helped most. Similar responses referred to grabbing registers, the interactive structure of the application, simplified block representations, and the visibility of decode and execute stages. Guidance also appeared frequently in this part of the questionnaire. Six participants referred to instructions, notes, hints, or panels as useful parts of the experience, while one participant identified the explanation before entering VR as particularly important for following the later task.

The responses describing confusion and possible improvements were more varied. Several comments concerned the local interaction sequence rather than the overall route between the six lesson phases described in Chapters~4 and~5. One participant wanted clearer indication of where individual inputs should be placed, another participant described the control signals and some input locations as unclear, and one participant requested more explicit prompts showing ``what to interact with, in what order, and what to do next''. Other comments focused on particular architecture tasks, including difficulties with register identification, ALU calculation, control-switch configuration, and the write-back stage. Practice-mode decode also produced requests for additional visual support, including colour coding and a drawing or segmentation aid for the encoded instruction.

Information presentation and physical navigation formed another recurring group of comments. One participant described the three-panel arrangement as containing too much text to follow comfortably, while another requested clearer panel labels, less scrolling, and slower joystick movement. One commented on repeated movement back to the register area and on guidance arrows that were not always helpful in the reduced-support task. Another reported a visibility problem in which some text-input areas were difficult to distinguish because the text and background were both light. These comments refer to the same panel, routing, and interaction systems described in Chapter~5, but record how those systems were experienced during the study.

Question~30 asked directly whether the VR interface had ever obstructed learning. Six participants described a specific interface or comfort-related problem, including nausea, dizziness, rotation and teleportation confusion, fast movement, object-placement difficulty, and text-input visibility. The remaining six answered No, Not really, Not at all, or otherwise stated that the interface had not interfered. The responses therefore included both participants who reported no meaningful interface interference and participants who identified physical comfort or interaction mechanics as sources of difficulty during the session.

Responses concerning future use of the system varied in the conditions participants attached to continued use. Six participants gave an unqualified positive response, five described future use as conditional on factors such as additional polish, reduced discomfort, greater novice support, or some prior understanding of the topic, and one participant answered No because dizziness outweighed the perceived benefit. One participant described the system as useful when the learner already knows ``a little bit about the topics'' and wants to clarify concepts, while another questioned whether the current level of terminology and prior knowledge expected by the prototype would make it more suitable for learners who already partly understand the material. A participant similarly made future use conditional on the system becoming more novice-friendly.

\section{Chapter Summary}

The participant sample entered the study with varied prior architecture knowledge, VR experience, confidence, and topic familiarity. The post-session questionnaire recorded frequent agreement on the usability, engagement, and perceived-learning items, while the separate discomfort item was endorsed by nine participants. Post-session knowledge performance averaged 75.56\% across Questions 11--25, with substantial variation between individual items. On the five repeated questions, the complete-case and available-item comparisons both recorded higher post-session descriptive averages than at baseline, alongside item-level cases in which performance remained unchanged or moved in both directions.

The open-ended responses and session notes added detail that was not captured by the structured measures. Participants repeatedly referred to hands-on interaction, visual representation, instructions, and hints when describing what helped them, while confusion and requested improvements concerned control signals, register and memory interactions, information density, local interaction sequencing, movement, and comfort. All 12 participants attempted Learning and Practice modes, two attempted Test mode, and the amount of researcher support recorded during the sessions ranged from minimal assistance to substantial guidance. Chapter~7 considers how these findings bear on the research question, the design choices established in Chapter~4, and the literature reviewed in Chapter~2.
