\begin{thesisabstract}
This dissertation investigates whether a bounded virtual reality learning environment can support students' understanding of selected single-cycle MIPS datapath concepts as a supplementary educational tool. Although standard diagrams and verbal explanations can introduce instruction execution, learners may still struggle to form a coherent mental model of how values, control choices, and architectural state changes relate across the datapath. In response, the dissertation presents a VR prototype that translates instruction execution into a guided spatial walkthrough organised around six learner-facing phases: Fetch, Decode, Execute, Memory, Write-Back, and Program Counter Update. Designed as a pedagogical abstraction rather than a cycle-accurate simulator, the prototype supports selected instructions across Learning mode, Practice mode, and Test mode.

The prototype was evaluated through an exploratory participant study using questionnaire responses, a post-session knowledge check, a limited baseline/post-session comparison, open-ended feedback, and researcher observations. Results showed generally positive usability and perceived-learning responses alongside recurring discomfort and interface-friction issues. Knowledge-check performance was generally strong, while the repeated comparison showed stronger descriptive performance on some items following the overall learning session. Because participants received a tailored refresher before entering the headset, these differences cannot be attributed to VR alone. Researcher observations showed some progression into reduced-support use alongside continuing difficulties with controls, information presentation, and selected lesson decisions.

The findings suggest that the prototype shows promise as a supplementary means of making selected computer architecture concepts more tangible and easier to follow, while remaining limited by the exploratory study design, participant comfort, and interface readability. The dissertation's main contribution is the development and evaluation of a pedagogical translation of single-cycle datapath reasoning into a sequential, learner-facing VR experience.
\end{thesisabstract}

\begin{thesisacknowledgments}
I owe this project first and foremost to my beloved parents, to whom I owe everything. Their love, more than I could ever repay, support from halfway across the world, and willingness to give me the opportunity to pursue this degree made all of this possible.

I thank my advisor, who helped pull me from one of the darkest periods of my life and set me on the path that eventually led me to this course and to Trinity.

I thank the friends I made along the way, who had my back throughout the academic year and helped keep me sane through the madness of the semesters. We pulled one another through difficult stretches, and even their quiet presence beside me while we worked made the long days feel less lonely.

I thank my supervisor, Gareth, for trusting me with this project, giving me the freedom to pursue it, and guiding me throughout a journey in which I was repeatedly given the chance to prove to myself what I was capable of.

More than anything, I thank the person I was a year and a half ago: the one who had grown accustomed to the dark, who was content to remain in the shadow of others, and who had begun to accept that this was simply where he belonged. He was afraid of taking even the first step forward, but he took it anyway. Somewhere along the road that followed, through the people around me and the responsibilities I was forced to grow into, I learned how to lead again. That first step is now so far behind me that I can no longer see where it began.

Teaching has always held a special place in my life. I once thought that to teach was simply to be a guiding light for someone else: to share what you know and help them find their way. Experience taught me that it is also an exchange. Every person you teach brings with them a perspective that changes your own, and in helping someone else understand, you are often made to understand anew yourself.

To teach is also to blaze a trail far enough that those who follow no longer need your light to find their way. If something of what you gave them remains, they carry those embers onward, beyond where you could have taken them yourself, leaving their own marks among a night already full of stars. That belief has stayed with me through every year I have spent teaching and learning, and, in many ways, it is the reason this dissertation became the project that it did.
\end{thesisacknowledgments}
