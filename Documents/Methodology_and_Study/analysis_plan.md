# Analysis Plan Draft

## 1. Purpose of the Analysis

The analysis should answer a practical question:

> What does the collected evidence suggest about usability, conceptual support, participant understanding, and the value of the prototype as a supplemental learning tool?

The goal is not to force large-sample statistical conclusions out of a small study.
The goal is to build a coherent evidence story from multiple sources.

---

## 2. Data Sources

The study produces five main data sources:

1. **Section A background data**
   - prior VR experience
   - prior computer architecture exposure
   - self-reported familiarity and confidence

2. **Short baseline probe**
   - selected approved pre-VR questions on key architecture concepts

3. **Researcher observation notes**
   - help level
   - confusion points
   - participant behavior during Learning / Practice / Test

4. **Post-session questionnaire sections**
   - Likert ratings
   - knowledge-check responses
   - open-ended feedback

5. **Session configuration metadata**
   - which instructions were attempted
   - which modes were completed
   - whether branching, memory, or arithmetic paths were experienced

---

## 3. Quantitative Analysis

The quantitative side should remain mostly descriptive.

That is completely acceptable for a project of this size.

### 3.1 Section A background profiling

Use Section A to summarize:

- how many participants had prior VR experience
- how many had studied architecture before
- how confident they felt before the session
- what topics they already found difficult

This matters because later participant performance should be interpreted in light of their starting familiarity.

### 3.2 Likert sections

For the Likert sections, compute:

- per-item frequencies
- means or medians where appropriate
- section-level summaries

These sections cover themes such as:

- usability
- engagement
- perceived learning
- confidence
- visual clarity / conceptual clarity

Given the likely participant count, descriptive reporting is stronger than pretending inferential testing is meaningful.

### 3.3 Knowledge check

For the knowledge check, summarize:

- total score per participant
- average score across participants
- most frequently missed questions
- whether errors cluster around specific concepts

This allows the dissertation to say which concepts remained difficult even after the VR session.

### 3.4 Baseline vs. post-session concept comparison

The short baseline subset should be compared descriptively against related post-session performance.

This does not need to be forced into a formal paired statistical test if the sample is too small.

Instead, report:

- how participants responded before VR on the baseline subset
- how they performed later on the knowledge-related items
- whether the pattern suggests improvement, clarification, or continued confusion

This creates a modest but defendable before/after comparison.

---

## 4. Qualitative Analysis

The qualitative side is where much of the nuance will come from.

Two main sources feed it:

- open-ended form responses
- researcher observation notes

### 4.1 Open-ended responses

These should be coded into recurring themes such as:

- what helped understanding
- what remained confusing
- navigation / control difficulties
- useful visualizations
- comments on guidance and pacing
- comparisons to lectures, diagrams, or other materials
- suggestions for improvement

### 4.2 Researcher notes

Observation notes should be used to capture:

- where participants hesitated
- where additional explanation was required
- whether they improved across modes
- whether confusion was conceptual or interaction-based

These notes should not replace participant voice, but they are useful for triangulation.

### 4.3 Coding approach

The ethics document already mentions NVivo, so the dissertation can state that:

- open-ended responses and observation notes are analyzed through thematic coding
- recurring themes are identified across sessions

Even if the coding is lightweight, the structure should be explicit.

---

## 5. Comparative Analysis Strategy

This is the part that needs to be explained carefully.

The study does **not** have a formal lecture-vs-VR control group.
So comparison should be handled in three bounded ways.

### 5.1 Self-reported comparison

Use the questionnaire items that explicitly ask whether the system:

- improved focus compared to standard learning materials
- would be preferred as a supplement to traditional teaching
- helped participants understand the topic better

These items give the most direct approved comparative data.

### 5.2 Baseline vs. post-session concept evidence

Use the short baseline subset plus the post-session knowledge check to discuss whether participants appeared more able to answer targeted architecture questions after the intervention.

### 5.3 Guided vs. reduced-guidance progression

Use behavior across Learning, Practice, and Test to show whether participants could complete tasks with less support over time.

This is an internal comparison within the system itself.

---

## 6. Recommended Tools

The analysis plan can still reference:

- **IBM SPSS Statistics** for descriptive quantitative summaries
- **NVivo** for thematic coding of open-ended responses and researcher notes

That stays aligned with the ethics submission and sounds methodologically coherent.

---

## 7. How Results Should Be Written Up

The results chapter should ideally be split into:

1. participant background profile
2. usability and experience findings
3. knowledge-check / conceptual findings
4. comparative findings against traditional materials
5. qualitative themes
6. synthesis and interpretation

This is cleaner than mixing everything together.

---

## 8. What the Analysis Should Ultimately Support

The analysis does not need to prove everything.
It only needs to support a clear interpretation:

- which parts of the prototype worked well
- which learning outcomes appeared to be supported
- where participants still struggled
- whether the system appears promising as a supplementary educational tool

That is enough for a strong dissertation argument.
